# TIME OUT — Full Script (as provided by the user, 2026-09-12)

## Storyline — Revised Opening & Cinematic Direction

### CINEMATIC LANGUAGE

The game establishes a visual and musical language immediately.

Gameplay is fast, physical, and demanding.

Cinematics are the opposite.

They slow the world down.

Camera movement becomes deliberate.

Lighting becomes dramatic.

Jack is often framed from unusual angles:

* Through windows
* Reflected in mirrors
* Seen from across a street
* Silhouetted against headlights
* Viewed from behind objects
* Extreme close-ups of his eyes or hands
* Low-angle shots of his motorcycle
* Wide shots showing how small he is within the town

Each cinematic has its own **electric-guitar motif**.

The guitar isn't constant background music.

It enters at important emotional moments.

A soft clean riff for Jack and his mother.

A lonely sustained guitar line when Jack thinks about leaving.

A faster blues-rock riff when Jack rides.

A darker distorted riff when the pendant appears.

As the story progresses, these motifs evolve.

Eventually the player recognizes the music before understanding why it is playing.

# DESIGN NOTES (2026-09-12, from the user)

## The escalation principle

Deliberately NOT "saving the world" from the beginning. Jack's initial motivation is much smaller:

> "I hate my life and I want something to happen."

Then something happens.

And it gets progressively bigger until Jack discovers that his boring little life is connected to an enormous temporal conflict.

Natural progression:

Small town → time travel → survival → exploration → mystery → family history → temporal conspiracy → collapsing timelines → final race.

## The signature move

The very first freeway jump becomes the game's signature move and eventually its final test. The player performed that maneuver dozens of hours earlier — but by the end they are doing it while the world changes underneath them.

## ESTABLISHED RULES (2026-09-12, from the user — treat as locked)

- **One geography, three timelines.** The past, present, and future occupy the same physical map coordinates. This is a foundational rule — the entire game is built around it.
- **Every scene is interactive.** Cutscenes are brief transitions, not long passive sequences.
- **Dexterity is fundamental.** Jack's movement, jumping, balancing, riding, and timing are the primary gameplay.
- **Time travel happens during gameplay** — including potentially while Jack is airborne.
- **Timeline resource roles:**
  - PRESENT: source of modern components and motorcycle upgrades.
  - MEDIEVAL: source of materials, fuel, repairs, and survival challenges.
  - FUTURE: source of advanced technology and major motorcycle upgrades.
- **The motorcycle evolves** throughout the game.
- **The bicycle has its own gameplay purpose** — precision and tighter environments (not just a slower bike).
- **Difficulty comes from combining mechanics**, not from bigger jumps or stronger enemies.
- **The freeway jump, precisely:** Jack uses the terrain beside the on-ramp as a launch hill to clear the entire freeway — he does NOT ride down the on-ramp.
- **Jack, precisely:** a bored 21-year-old who thinks he's trapped in a small town. His obsession with Back to the Future makes actual time travel hilariously ironic.

## CORE THESIS (2026-09-12, from the user)

**Time travel isn't a puzzle mechanic that interrupts the action. Time travel IS the action.**

## The signature mechanic: one location, three times

The player learns the geography of the world across time. Example — the freeway crossing:

**PRESENT — 1999:** Jack sees the freeway and the grassy embankment beside the on-ramp. The player learns "this is my jump." Build speed, climb, crest, launch.

**MID-AIR:** The player activates the pendant. FLASH.

**MEDIEVAL:** The freeway is gone. Jack is suddenly flying over a massive medieval ravine. The trajectory is exactly the same. Control the bike in the air, land on the opposite side.

**LATER — FUTURE:** That same location is now a gigantic collapsed megastructure. The jump that was a freeway stunt becomes a completely different challenge.

Late-game version of the same jump: "Hit the embankment at exactly the right speed → launch → rotate the motorcycle → activate the pendant at the apex → transition to medieval → avoid the castle tower → activate the pendant again → transition to the future → land on a moving platform."

Difficulty without artificial difficulty: the challenge comes from combining skills the player already learned.

## One world, not three levels

There are NOT three separate versions of every level. There is one world; three versions of it exist simultaneously. The player builds a mental map — "I know what's at this location" — then must ask:

- "What is here in 1999?"
- "What was here 1,000 years ago?"
- "What will be here 1,000 years from now?"

That question IS the game's signature mechanic.

## Learning progression

1. Player learns the environment.
2. Player learns the motorcycle.
3. Player learns the three timelines.
4. Player combines all three.

## The Back to the Future joke (character beat)

Early: Jack thinks time travel is awesome because he's obsessed with the movie.
Mid: he's experiencing actual time travel.
Late, after nearly getting killed for the fiftieth time:

**JACK:** "Okay. The movie made this look way easier."

Protagonist, world, and mechanic all reinforce each other.

## VISUAL BASELINE (2026-09-12, from the user)

Target: **Prince of Persia: The Lost Crown** (PC) — researched 2026-09-12.

What that game actually is, visually:

- **2.5D, not flat 2D.** 3D character models and 3D environments with side-scrolling gameplay. The art director explicitly rejected full cel-shaded/flattened anime looks *because depth matters* — the camera swoops and swings dramatically (e.g., into close-ups during special attacks), which only works with real 3D.
- **Stylized, never photoreal.** Anime-inspired (Dragon Ball, One Piece, Demon Slayer touchstones), plus comic-book and street-art influences; a "nekketsu-shonen" vibe. Described as tactile and grounded stylization (Star Wars: Rebels / Sea of Thieves territory).
- **Signature = 2D elements mixed into 3D.** Illustrated/graphic 2D effects layered over 3D representation.
- **Painterly, hand-painted-feel backgrounds.** Storybook-meets-mythic-epic; detailed layered backdrops with strong parallax; vibrant, distinct biomes.
- **Fluid, momentum-heavy animation.** Movement conveys weight and speed.
- **Dramatic camera as a feature.** Swoops in on big moments with bursts of bright color.

What this means for TIME OUT:

- Build 3D (models, depth, parallax layers) on a side-scrolling plane — never flat sprites.
- Stylized art direction over realism: achievable and age-proof.
- 2D graphic effects (speed lines, impact bursts, timeline-flash frames) layered over the 3D scene — directly supports "the sound of time itself" having a *visual* equivalent.
- The camera is a storytelling tool: the freeway jump / timeline transitions are the natural "camera swoop" moments.
- Each timeline gets its own palette and background treatment (present = warm nostalgic, medieval = earthy/painterly, future = cold/processed) — the art reinforces the guitar motifs.
- Honest scope note: The Lost Crown was a full Ubisoft Montpellier team. Asset-for-asset parity isn't the goal; matching its *visual language* (stylized 2.5D + painterly depth + dramatic camera) is — and stylized is the most solo-dev-achievable direction there is.
